THE HEADLINE FACT
-----------------
Encode Club, Filecoin-themed hackathon. February 2025. Aaron
won $2,500 across two bounties — Storacha and Lilypad. His
first hackathon. Built a sign-language detection web app
backed by decentralised storage and decentralised compute.

Aaron's caveat on the win:
"I did have probably much more time as I had nothing else I
was working on and a great foundation from the uni CNN and
mediapipe model work. It was a great feeling and the family
were happy."


WHAT ENCODE CLUB IS
-------------------
A hackathon-hosting organisation that runs events across AI,
web3, and crypto. The Filecoin hackathon Aaron joined was
specifically about building on web3 decentralised storage and
compute technologies. Each provider in the stack — Filecoin,
Storacha, Lilypad, Akave, others — put up their own bounty.

Format: 2 weeks of seminars and lessons → 2 weeks of building
→ submission → judging.


WHERE AARON WAS BEFORE THE HACKATHON
------------------------------------
Aaron's words:
"In February I was just rotting, not knowing what to do
because I had just come out of the ecommerce journey and
unsure about the future."

Context: this is post-Aviini. Aviini (the e-commerce business
covered in video_phase2_brief.txt) had just ended via IP /
fake-sue concerns around January 2025 — 18 months of work
ending unwillingly. The gap between Aviini ending and the
hackathon starting was literally days, not weeks.

Important framing for the editor: this is NOT a depressive-trough
story. Aaron's relationship to quitting back then was
behavioural — he'd actually quit things rather than agonise
over whether to. So Feb 2025 reads as "in motion but aimless"
rather than "stuck in a hole". The viewer can relate to "I just
lost something I'd been building, what now?"

THE PROJECT CHOICE (the honest version)
---------------------------------------
The build: a sign language detection web app where users could
contribute new ASL training data. That data would be stored on
decentralised storage (Storacha), and the model could later be
retrained on it. Inference itself ran on decentralised compute
(Lilypad).

Aaron's confession on why this idea — and how it got picked so
fast:
"This was kinda a hack because the CNN and the mediapipe
approaches were things I had done for a university project. I
had the CNN one from the get go and I had to upgrade the
mediapipe approach because the accuracy was better. Both were
terrible accuracy wise — they would guess the wrong letter —
but the purpose was to demonstrate the use case rather than
optimising the model."

Why this idea won the team's vote without much debate:
"It could hit multiple bounties but not too many as to spread
ourselves too thin."

Editor note: this is the "demo over perfect" beat. The model
was bad on purpose. The win came from showing the use-case
clearly, not from optimising the underlying ML. Worth flagging
to the audience as a supporting lesson without making it the
spine.


THE LEARNING WEEKS (the seminars Aaron didn't really attend)
-----------------------------------------------------------
2 weeks of provider seminars and onboarding sessions. Aaron's
honest version of what he actually did during them:
"Not really looking into anything but playing around with
cursor and gpt at the time with these different technologies
and trying to set them up and get them to work."

Per-provider impressions:
- Filecoin: "No clue how anything worked with this node concept
  and thinking how the hell am I going to do anything in this
  hackathon."
- Storacha: "The most normal API to setup and was fairly
  intuitive."
- Lilypad: Made him learn Docker concepts — especially building
  images so random computers in the network could run the
  model. The biggest grey area.


THE BUILD WEEKS
---------------
Coding stack: Aaron + Cursor + GPT. He flags this as his
"early entry into agentic coding" — Feb 2025 was very early
for that style of working.

Aaron's words on the texture of the build:
"This was when I enjoyed coding more because of all these
tools allowing me to get to the satisfying outcome of a
successful result faster. I do remember the phase of the first
day not understanding anything at all and then the next
grasping things and moving with more clarity. The mind is
weird."

The Lilypad / Docker grind: slow steady grind across many
days, no specific "throw the laptop" peak. The emotional
centre of the build is the day-1 → day-2 mental flip, not a
breakthrough night.

WHAT THE WIN ACTUALLY DID (the year after — the lesson)
-------------------------------------------------------
This is the spine of the video's takeaway, and Aaron's answer
to it course-corrects mid-sentence in a way that's the most
honest version of the truth.

Aaron's words (including the self-correction):
"It gave me more confidence in the things I was building had
value — but tbh it did not. It more made me think I can
showcase my value to others through that hackathon win. It
was a real world proof of my value. It still did not solidify
the belief that my other endeavours were valuable. Joining
Cogram was what did that for my pursuit — like LINQ and
ScrollBuddy and the other failed web apps."

The distinction the brief is asking Kharel to surface:
- The hackathon delivered EXTERNAL validation: a CV line, a
  dollar number, the dopamine of a stranger's "you won".
- The hackathon did NOT deliver INTERNAL validation: the
  question "is what I'm pursuing actually worth pursuing"
  stayed open.
- INTERNAL validation came later, from sustained work — the
  Cogram contractor relationship.

Editor framing for the audience persona: do not let the video
imply a single hackathon win cures self-doubt. The honest
takeaway is closer to "one win is a data point, not a
diagnosis. Keep going."
